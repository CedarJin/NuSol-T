Estimating Ingredient Compositions from
Mandatory Nutrition Labels

A Validation Study on 3,734 USDA FNDDS Multi-Ingredient Recipes
Using Constrained Optimization with Slack-Variable Quadratic Programming
NuSol-T Project · July 2026

1   Introduction

Processed and packaged foods dominate modern diets, yet their exact ingredient proportions remain proprietary. A food label reveals what is inside (the ingredient list, in descending order by weight) and how nutritious it is (the Nutrition Facts panel), but not how much of each ingredient is present. Recovering this hidden information would enable researchers to estimate a product's full nutrient profile beyond the mandatory label nutrients, calculate its environmental footprint, or model the health impact of ingredient substitutions — all from publicly available data alone.

Bohn et al. (2022) demonstrated the feasibility of this "inverse recipe" problem on 1,804 German supermarket products, achieving a 76% success rate using only the EU-mandated Big7 nutrients and the German Nutrient Database (Bundeslebensmittelschlüssel). Their validation on products with known recipes showed an average deviation of just 0.9% from true ingredient compositions.

This study extends Bohn et al. (2022) in three ways:
(1) We validate the method on a much larger and more diverse dataset — 3,734 USDA FNDDS recipes with known ground-truth ingredient fractions.
(2) We incorporate 13 FDA-mandated label nutrients (vs. 7 EU Big7 nutrients) and additional structural constraints (ingredient order within the ≤2% group, two-percent rule).
(3) We address practical data challenges: fortificant filtering, kJ→kcal unit correction, and raw-to-cooked yield factor estimation via energy conservation.

The core scientific question is: given only the information available to a consumer reading a food label — the Nutrition Facts panel and the ordered ingredient list — can we reliably recover the mass fraction of each ingredient?

2   The Inverse Recipe Problem

2.1   Forward Model: Linear Mixing

A manufactured food is a physical mixture. Each ingredient i contributes its nutrient profile (per 100 g) in proportion to its mass fraction xi in the finished product. Under the assumption of mass and nutrient conservation (no chemical transformation beyond water loss):

Nutrientfinal, j = Σi xi · Nutrienti,j   for each nutrient j = 1, …, m
subject to   Σi xi = 1,   xi ≥ 0

This is the same linear mixing assumption used in food composition databases (Greenfield & Southgate, 2003) and in Bohn et al. (2022).

2.3   Why It's an Inverse Problem

The forward direction is straightforward: given a recipe x, predict the label nutrients as A·x. The inverse direction — recovering x from observed b — is underdetermined when n > m (more ingredients than observed nutrients). Additional constraints from FDA labeling regulations and food science domain knowledge are required to narrow the solution space.

3   Methods

3.1   Ingredient-to-Nutrient Mapping

Each ingredient name from the FNDDS recipe is mapped to its nutrient profile via a four-level fallback strategy using USDA FoodData Central:

L1: FNDDS foodCode (self-lookup)→ L2: Foundation Foods ndbNumber (2026)→ L3: SR Legacy ndbNumber (2018)→ L4: Name-based fuzzy search

Pure nutrient additives without food composition data (fortificants: Vitamin D, Calcium, Iron, Folic Acid, etc. as ingredient) are identified and excluded from the ingredient list, with affected label nutrients (e.g., calcium dominated by fortification) excluded from observations.

3.2   Observations: Simulating the Nutrition Facts Panel

For each of the 13 FDA-mandated label nutrients (Energy, Protein, Total Fat, Saturated Fat, Carbohydrate, Fiber, Sugars, Cholesterol, Sodium, Calcium, Iron, Potassium, Vitamin D), we construct symmetric ±10% observation intervals around the FNDDS final product nutrient value. This reflects the inherent uncertainty in both label values (FDA rounding rules) and database values (biological variation in raw ingredients).

3.3   Constraint Hierarchy

LevelConstraintTypeScientific Basis
P0Mass balance: Σ xi = 1 Hard Conservation of mass (physics)
P1Ingredient order: xi ≥ xi+1 (main group only) Hard FDA 21 CFR 101.4: ingredients declared in descending order
P1Two-percent rule: xi ≤ 0.02 for ≤2% group Hard FDA labeling regulation
P2Nutrient intervals: A·x ∈ [0.9b, 1.1b] Soft Label-database congruence; regulatory tolerances (FDA 21 CFR 101.9)

Hard constraints must be satisfied exactly. Soft constraints permit bounded violation penalized quadratically in the objective function via slack variables (Boyd & Vandenberghe, 2004).

3.4   Slack-Variable Quadratic Programming (Point Solver)

Soft nutrient-interval constraints are reformulated with slack variables sk ≥ 0 that absorb bounded violations:

minimize   Σk wk · sk²
subject to   Ahard·x = bhard
        A·x + s ≥ lo
        A·x − s ≤ hi
        s ≥ 0

The quadratic penalty w·s² has increasing marginal cost, encouraging the solver to distribute small deviations across multiple nutrients rather than concentrate a large error on one. Implemented via SciPy's SLSQP algorithm (Nocedal & Wright, 2006) with 10 randomized restarts to avoid local optima.

3.5   Feasible Bounds via Linear Programming (Bounds Solver)

Independently, we compute each ingredient's feasible minimum and maximum fraction by solving 2n linear programs (min xi and max xi subject to all hard constraints) via the HiGHS simplex solver (Huangfu & Hall, 2018). This provides a feasible range for each ingredient — the point estimate must lie within these bounds.

3.6   Data Quality Corrections

kJ → kcal Energy Unit Correction
SR Legacy occasionally stores energy values in kJ while labeling them as "kcal". Detected via Atwater's 4-4-9 formula (Atwater & Woods, 1896): if reported energy exceeds 3× the macronutrient-derived estimate, the value is divided by 4.184.
Yield Factor: Raw → Cooked Correction
SR Legacy stores raw ingredient nutrient profiles, but FNDDS final products are often cooked. Cooking causes moisture loss, concentrating nutrients. Because energy is conserved (only water evaporates), the yield factor (Ecooked / Eraw) estimates the concentration ratio using raw weight fractions — no ground-truth leakage.

5.5   Sensitivity Analysis: Observation Interval Width

To assess how the choice of interval width affects solver performance, we varied the observation intervals from ±5% to ±20% around the FNDDS label value on the 197-recipe validation set. Wider intervals make the problem easier to solve (higher success rate) but reduce precision (higher MAE). The ±10% default represents a balance point.

Why ±10%? ±5% is too tight — USDA ingredient data have natural biological variation of 10–15%, making 5% intervals narrower than the data uncertainty itself (1 recipe fails). Widening from ±10% to ±15% gains only 0.5% success rate (196→197) at the cost of 38% higher median MAE (1.89→2.61 pp). ±10% is also stricter than the FDA compliance tolerance of ±20% for Class II nutrients, ensuring that successful solutions represent genuinely self-consistent recipes rather than merely permissive intervals.

6   When Does the Method Fail?

The 17 irrecoverable failures (0.5% of 3,734) fall into three categories that illuminate the method's boundaries:

6.1   Category 1: Single-Ingredient Foods Misclassified as Multi-Ingredient

FNDDS lists pure nutrient additives (e.g., Vitamin D 0.004 g added to 100 g yogurt) as separate "ingredients." After filtering these fortificants, only one real ingredient remains — the problem reduces to x = 1.0 trivially. Six recipes: yogurt (whole, low-fat, nonfat, baby), shredded wheat, and calcium-fortified orange juice. These are not genuine multi-ingredient inverse problems.

6.2   Category 2: Nutritionally Indistinguishable Cultivars

Three recipes blend different cultivars or ripeness stages of the same species: banana (ripe 90% + overripe 10%), tomato (red 60% + roma 30% + grape 10%), and onion (yellow 60% + white 30% + red 10%). The nutrient profiles of these variants are chemically indistinguishable — any weighted combination of them produces the same predicted nutrient vector. This is a fundamental limitation of the linear mixing model (van der Voet et al., 2011).

6.3   Category 3: Shellfish, Organ Meats, and Complex Combinations

Eight recipes remain unsolved despite yield factor correction. These include clams, shrimp, hog maws (pork stomach), turkey with skin, bagels with raisins, and canned cooked tomatoes. Likely causes: (a) SR Legacy organ meat data quality is lower than for muscle meats; (b) 2-ingredient recipes have only one degree of freedom after mass balance; (c) nutrient overlap between similar ingredients.

Key insight: The method succeeds when ingredients have chemically distinct nutrient profiles — which is true for the vast majority of processed foods (different ingredient categories: grain vs. fat vs. sugar vs. protein source). It fails when ingredients are nutritionally similar, which is a mathematical identifiability limit, not an algorithmic one.

The higher success rate in our study is attributable to three factors: (1) the use of 13 nutrients rather than 7, providing more independent equations; (2) FNDDS recipes have known, validated ingredient-to-nutrient mappings via USDA codes (the German BLS may have sparser coverage); and (3) the yield factor correction addresses raw-to-cooked mismatches that would otherwise cause failures.

8   Discussion

8.1   Scientific Significance

This study provides the largest validation to date of the inverse recipe estimation approach first proposed by Westrich et al. (1994) and recently operationalized by Bohn et al. (2022). With 3,734 ground-truth recipes and a 94.9% auto-pipeline success rate (99.5% with systematic corrections), we demonstrate that the method is robust across a wide range of food categories, ingredient counts, and nutrient profiles.

8.2   Practical Implications

Reliable ingredient fraction estimation from label data alone enables:

  • Nutrient expansion: estimating 150+ nutrients beyond the 13–15 mandatory label nutrients, by combining estimated fractions with full USDA nutrient profiles (Bohn et al., 2022).
  • Environmental impact assessment: calculating carbon footprint, water use, and land use of individual food products from their ingredient composition.
  • Dietary monitoring: large-scale analysis of the food supply for public health surveillance, reformulation tracking, and regulatory compliance.
  • Product development: reverse-engineering competitor products for benchmarking and reformulation in the food industry.

8.3   Limitations

  • The method assumes linear mixing without chemical transformation during processing. Fermented, deep-fried, or heavily heat-treated products may violate this assumption.
  • Ingredient-to-database mapping requires standardized ingredient names; branded food labels use marketing names that need additional parsing and mapping infrastructure.
  • The current point solver (SLSQP) may fail to converge on nearly-flat optimization landscapes (typ. 2–3 ingredient recipes). An interior-point solver (IPOPT) may improve robustness.
