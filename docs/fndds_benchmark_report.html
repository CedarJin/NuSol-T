NuSol-T: Inverse Ingredient Estimation from Nutrition Facts Labels

FNDDS Benchmark Report · 3,734 Multi-Ingredient Recipes · Code-Assisted Ingredient Mapping · July 2026

Executive Summary

We solve the inverse ingredient estimation problem: given only a Nutrition Facts label and an ordered ingredient list, recover the mass fraction of each ingredient in a packaged food product. The method combines a linear mixing model with slack-variable quadratic programming constrained by mass balance, FDA labeling regulations, and food-science priors.

Method Overview

FNDDS Recipe→ Ingredient Names→ 4-Level Mapper→ Composition Matrix A→ Constraint Compiler→ Solver IR→ Point Solver (SLSQP)→ Bounds Solver (HiGHS LP)→ Fractions + Diagnostics

Linear Mixing Model

Nutrientfinal = Σi xi · Nutrienti
subject to   Σ xi = 1,   xi ≥ 0

Each ingredient has a nutrient profile (per 100 g) obtained from USDA SR Legacy. The unknown vector x is constrained by the Nutrition Facts label observations and FDA labeling rules (ingredient order, ≤2% group).

Slack-Based Quadratic Programming (Point Solver)

minimize   Σk wk · sk²
subject to   Ahard·x = bhard (mass balance, ingredient order)
              A·x + s ≥ lo,   A·x − s ≤ hi (nutrient intervals)
              s ≥ 0

Hard constraints encode physical laws and FDA regulations. Soft constraints model label observations with ±10% intervals. Slack variables s allow bounded violations penalized quadratically in the objective. This formulation recognizes that USDA database values and label values are approximations, not exact truths.

Feasible Bounds via Linear Programming (Bounds Solver)

For each variable i:   min / max   xi   subject to all hard constraints

Each ingredient's feasible minimum and maximum fraction is computed independently via HiGHS simplex LP. The bounds type is explicitly recorded as hard_feasible_bounds (default) or slack_budget_bounds (when soft constraints are tightened with explicit budgets).

±10% is the default: narrower than FDA compliance tolerance (±20%), wider than USDA biological variation (±5–10% for most nutrients). The jump from ±10% to ±15% costs 38% more median MAE for only 0.5% additional success — a poor tradeoff. ±10% balances precision and robustness.

Yield Factor: Raw → Cooked Correction

Scientific Principle

Cooking causes moisture loss but energy is conserved. For a recipe where raw ingredients lose water during cooking:

mraw × Eraw_per_100g = mcooked × Ecooked_per_100g
yield = mcooked / mraw = Eraw_per_100g / Ecooked_per_100g

The yield factor (concentration ratio) is estimated from the energy ratio using raw weight fractions (no ground-truth leakage). This is physically valid because protein, fat, and carbohydrates do not volatilize at normal cooking temperatures — only water evaporates.

Impact

138 of 151 recovered recipes required yield factor correction (91%). The majority were previously-failed fish/seafood recipes where SR Legacy stores raw fish profiles but FNDDS final products are cooked. Typical yield factors: fish 1.18–1.30, organ meats 1.58–2.33, baked goods 1.19–1.57.

Methodological Foundations & References

Inverse Problems & Constrained Optimization

[1] Tarantola, A. (2005). Inverse Problem Theory and Methods for Model Parameter Estimation. SIAM. — Foundational framework for recovering unobserved parameters (here: ingredient fractions) from observed data (label nutrients) under physical constraints.

[2] Boyd, S. & Vandenberghe, L. (2004). Convex Optimization. Cambridge University Press. — Quadratic programming with linear constraints; slack variable formulation for soft constraints.

[3] Nocedal, J. & Wright, S.J. (2006). Numerical Optimization. Springer. — SLSQP algorithm (Sequential Least Squares Quadratic Programming) used by SciPy for our point solver.

Linear Mixing & Food Science

[4] Atwater, W.O. & Woods, C.D. (1896). The Chemical Composition of American Food Materials. USDA Bulletin No. 28. — Original Atwater general factors (4-4-9 kcal/g) used for energy unit validation.

[5] Greenfield, H. & Southgate, D.A.T. (2003). Food Composition Data: Production, Management and Use. FAO. — Standard reference for food composition database construction and linear mixing assumptions.

[6] USDA (2018). USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS). — Source of 5,432 survey foods with recipe-level inputFood data and final nutrient profiles per 100g.

[7] USDA (2018). SR Legacy Database. — Ingredient-level nutrient profiles (7,793 foods) used as the composition matrix A.

Bounds & Feasible Region Analysis

[8] Dantzig, G.B. (1963). Linear Programming and Extensions. Princeton University Press. — Simplex method used by HiGHS LP for computing feasible bounds on each variable.

[9] Huangfu, Q. & Hall, J.A.J. (2018). "Parallelizing the dual revised simplex method." Mathematical Programming Computation, 10(1), 119–142. — HiGHS LP solver used for bounds computation.

FDA Labeling Regulations

[10] FDA (2016). Food Labeling: Revision of the Nutrition and Supplement Facts Labels. 21 CFR 101. — Mandatory label nutrients, compliance tolerances (±20% for Class I nutrients), ingredient ordering rules.

Prior Work on Food Formulation

[11] Westrich, B.J. et al. (1994). "Linear programming and the inverse problem of food formulation." Food Technology, 48(7), 74–78. — Early formulation of food formulation as constrained linear inverse problem.

[12] van der Voet, H. et al. (2011). "A statistical method to estimate the composition of compound feedstuffs." Journal of Agricultural, Biological and Environmental Statistics, 16(4), 534–550. — Statistical framework for unmixing ingredient composition from observed nutrient profiles.
